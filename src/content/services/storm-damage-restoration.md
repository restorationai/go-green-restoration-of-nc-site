## What Storm Damage Restoration actually involves

Storm damage is rarely one problem. It's usually wind, water, and debris arriving together, and each one needs a different response. A tree limb punching through a roof deck creates an open path for rain even after the storm passes. Wind-driven rain can push water sideways under siding and shingles without any visible hole at all, soaking sheathing and insulation that looks fine from inside the house. Downed power lines or flooded crawlspaces add electrical and structural hazards on top of the water problem.

Our crews stabilize the structure first: emergency tarping over roof openings, board-up for broken windows or doors, and water extraction from affected rooms. From there it's the same moisture tracking and structural drying used on any water loss, but with an added layer of documentation because storm claims get more adjuster scrutiny than a simple pipe burst. Equipment includes heavy-duty tarps rated for wind, plywood board-up materials, extraction pumps, and the same air movers and dehumidifiers used in standard water drying, sized to the affected square footage.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization.** Tarping over roof damage, board-up on broken windows or doors, and shutting off power or water to the affected area if it's unsafe.
2. **Water intrusion assessment.** We check not just where water is visibly pooling but where wind-driven rain may have pushed moisture into wall cavities, attic insulation, and subfloor, using moisture meters and thermal imaging.
3. **Debris and tree coordination.** For trees on or against the structure, we coordinate removal with the priority of preventing further structural movement or roof collapse before extraction and drying begin.
4. **Structural drying and moisture mapping.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed and monitored daily until materials read dry, not just damp-to-the-touch.
5. **Documentation and claim support.** Photos of wind damage, debris impact, and water intrusion are logged before and during tarping and drying, since this is often the evidence an adjuster needs to separate storm damage from pre-existing conditions.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The biggest mistake we see is treating storm damage as a roofing problem only. A tarp stops new rain from getting in, but it does nothing for the water that already soaked into insulation and sheathing before the tarp went up. If that moisture isn't tracked and dried, mold can start colonizing within 24 to 48 hours in warm, humid conditions, which is most of the year in this part of North Carolina.

Another common gap is documentation timing. Adjusters distinguish between wind damage (typically covered) and flood damage from rising water (often excluded or requiring separate flood coverage), and that distinction depends heavily on photos taken before cleanup starts. Tarping or removing debris without documenting it first can cost a homeowner part of their claim. A thorough response photographs damage, measures moisture before and after drying, and keeps a clear record of what was wind versus what may have been ground or surface water.
