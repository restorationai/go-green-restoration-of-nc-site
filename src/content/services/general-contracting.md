A water-damaged kitchen that's been gutted down to the studs doesn't fix itself once the drying equipment leaves. Neither does a bathroom where subfloor rot finally forced the issue, or a fire-damaged addition that's been sitting under a tarp since the insurance claim closed. Somewhere between the demolition and the day you get your house back, someone has to frame it, wire it, plumb it, and finish it. That's the gap most restoration companies hand off to a stranger. We don't.

## What renovations, remodels and general contracting actually involves

This isn't paint-and-trim work. Post-damage rebuilds in Middlesex and the surrounding county typically start with structures that were opened up for drying, demolition, or code reasons and need to be put back together to current building standards, not just patched. That can mean reframing a wall after water-damaged studs were cut out, replacing subfloor that failed moisture testing, running new electrical where a fire damaged the original wiring, or a full kitchen or bathroom remodel when the original fixtures and cabinetry were removed as part of the loss. Timelines vary widely: a single bathroom rebuild after a supply line failure might run three to five weeks once permits clear, while a kitchen remodel tied to a larger fire or water claim can stretch past two months depending on cabinet lead times and inspection scheduling. The work itself spans framing, drywall, electrical, plumbing rough-in, flooring, cabinetry, and finish carpentry, usually coordinated under one scope rather than six separate trade contracts.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work.** We document existing conditions, measure the space, and write a detailed scope that separates what's covered under an insurance claim from what's homeowner-elected upgrade work, so there's no confusion later about who's paying for what.
2. **Permits and plan review.** Structural, electrical, and plumbing permits get pulled through the local building department before any framing starts. Skipping this step is the single most common reason a rebuild stalls mid-project.
3. **Rough-in and inspection.** Framing goes up first, followed by electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in. Each trade gets inspected and signed off before we close up walls, which protects you if a problem surfaces later.
4. **Finish work.** Drywall, flooring, cabinetry, tile, paint, and fixtures go in last. This is where a kitchen or bathroom remodel actually starts looking like the finished product, and where material selections and lead times matter most to the schedule.
5. **Final walkthrough.** We walk the completed space with you against the original scope, confirm punch-list items are closed out, and make sure final inspections are filed with the county.

## What separates a good general contracting response from a bad one

The most common failure point isn't the finish work, it's what happens before drywall goes back up. A contractor in a hurry to close out a claim will sometimes close in framing before an electrical or plumbing inspection has signed off, which creates real problems if an adjuster or inspector later asks for proof of what's behind the wall. Another frequent miss: treating a post-damage rebuild like a cosmetic remodel and skipping moisture verification on framing and subfloor before closing it up, which can trap residual moisture from the original loss behind new drywall. Adjusters scrutinizing a rebuild scope typically want to see line-item documentation tying each repair back to the original damage, a permit trail, and photos at each stage, not just a final invoice. A scope that's vague about what's damage-related versus betterment work also tends to get kicked back for revision, which delays payment and delays your project.

## What does renovations, remodels and general contracting cost?

Costs vary enormously depending on square footage, whether the work is tied to an insurance claim, and material selections, so the ranges below are typical industry figures, not a quote. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Middlesex sits in a region with a mix of older farmhouse-era homes and newer builds, which means rebuild scopes often run into legacy wiring, plaster-and-lath walls, or crawlspace foundations that need extra attention during framing and inspection. Humid North Carolina summers also mean any rebuild following a water loss needs verified dry framing before closing walls, since trapped moisture in this climate has more opportunity to turn into a mold problem down the line.
