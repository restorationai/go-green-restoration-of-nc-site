## What Flood Damage Restoration actually involves

Flood damage restoration deals with water that originated outside the home's plumbing system: overland flooding, storm surge, a failed sump pump during heavy rain, or groundwater forced up through a slab. The water is almost always classified as Category 3 (grossly contaminated) under the IICRC S500 standard, which means porous materials it touches, carpet pad, drywall below the flood line, insulation, baseboards, generally cannot be dried in place and have to come out. Crews work with submersible pumps and truck-mount extractors for standing water, then move to air movers, low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers, and antimicrobial application once the bulk water is gone. On a single-story home with a few inches of floodwater, expect demolition and extraction within the first day, then 3 to 5 days of monitored drying before rebuild can start.

## Our process

1. **Water categorization and safety check.** We confirm the water source and contamination level before anyone steps inside, since flood water can hide electrical hazards, structural weakening, or sewage backflow.
2. **Extraction and controlled demolition.** Standing water comes out first with pumps and extractors, followed by removal of saturated drywall (typically cut 12 to 18 inches above the flood line), ruined flooring, and insulation that can't be salvaged.
3. **Muck-out and antimicrobial treatment.** Silt, mud, and debris left behind by floodwater get cleared and surfaces are treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial to address contamination before drying begins.
4. **Structural drying with daily monitoring.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run on a calculated drying plan, with moisture readings logged in wall cavities, subfloor, and framing until materials hit dry standard, not just "looks dry."
5. **Documentation handoff.** Photos, moisture logs, and a scope of work are compiled for your adjuster or flood insurance carrier before any rebuild begins.

## What separates a good flood damage response from a bad one

The most common shortcut is drying floodwater like it's a clean-water leak, running fans over contaminated carpet pad instead of removing it. That leaves bacteria and organic matter trapped in materials that will smell and degrade for months. Another frequent miss is stopping the drywall cut at the visible waterline instead of a few inches above it, which ignores wicking, the way water climbs through gypsum board by capillary action well past where it looks wet. Adjusters reviewing a flood claim look for moisture-mapping documentation at the start and end of drying, photos of contaminated materials before removal, and a clear line between what was dried in place versus demolished. Crews that skip daily monitoring often sign off on "dry" too early, and secondary mold claims a few weeks later are far more expensive than finishing the job right the first time.
