## What Counts as Water Damage, and How Serious Is It?

Water damage is classified two ways: by what's in the water (Category) and by how much of the structure it soaked (Class). A burst supply line is Category 1, clean water. A dishwasher backup or long-standing Category 1 water left more than 48 hours gets reclassified as Category 2, gray water. Sewage backups and floodwater from outside the home are Category 3, black water, which the [IICRC](https://www.iicrc.org) treats as contaminated and requiring different protective equipment during cleanup.

Class refers to the extent of absorption, not the water source. Class 1 affects a small area with low-permeance materials. Class 4 means water has soaked into hardwood, plaster, or concrete and will need specialty drying methods and more time. A single-room leak from a window AC unit is a very different job than a slab leak that's been running under the subfloor for a week, even though both are technically "water damage."

## What Should You Do in the First 24 Hours?

Stop the water source, then remove standing water and start air movement, in that order. Every hour water sits on flooring or inside wall cavities increases the chance it migrates into materials that are expensive to dry, like subfloor and insulation.

Shut off the water at the fixture or the main if you can't isolate it. Cut power to any outlets or fixtures in the affected area if water is near them, and don't do this yourself if it means stepping into standing water near an outlet. Then move furniture and rugs off wet flooring, run a wet/dry vac on standing water, and open windows or run box fans and a dehumidifier if you have one. Photograph everything before you move or discard anything; your insurance adjuster will want it.

## Will Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage Repairs?

Most NC homeowners policies cover water damage that's sudden and accidental, like a burst pipe or a failed water heater, but exclude damage from gradual leaks or deferred maintenance. The distinction the adjuster is checking for is whether the water loss happened suddenly or built up slowly over weeks or months.

A pipe that freezes and splits overnight is typically covered. A slow drip under a sink that's been staining the cabinet floor for six months usually isn't, because the policy treats that as a maintenance issue the homeowner should have caught. Flood damage from rising water outside the home, think Tar River or Neuse River overflow after heavy rain, is excluded from standard homeowners policies entirely and requires separate flood insurance through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance).

Documentation is what moves a claim along. Photos of the source, a moisture log showing drying progress, and a written scope of work from whoever handles the mitigation all support the claim.
